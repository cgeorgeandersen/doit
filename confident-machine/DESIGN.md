# The Confident Machine: design document

An interactive essay on how to live and work with AI. This file records the plan,
the decisions behind it, and every significant assumption, so an editor can see
why the piece is built the way it is.

## 1. Thesis

Modern AI is a brilliant, fluent, confident machine that is sometimes wrong in ways
you can't see. The most valuable skill of the next decade is not prompting. It is
**calibrated trust**: knowing when to rely on AI, when to check it, and when to keep
a task for yourself.

Every section has to move a reader along one path:

1. **See the problem.** Fluency is not truth (Opening).
2. **Understand the cause.** The machine predicts likely words; nothing in that
   process checks facts (Chapter 1).
3. **Measure the gap.** Confidence and accuracy come apart, for people and machines
   (Chapter 2).
4. **Map the unevenness.** Ability is jagged, and our sense of "difficulty" is a poor
   guide (Chapter 3).
5. **Act on it.** A practical rule: use AI where checking is cheaper than doing
   (Chapter 4).
6. **Scale it up.** When one system makes thousands of decisions, small errors become
   policy (Chapter 5).
7. **Stay current.** The boundary moves, and so do the facts about it (Chapter 6).
8. **Choose.** Decide what to keep doing yourself, and leave with your own rules (Close).

Tone: calm, curious, anti-hype. No doom, no evangelism. Contested questions are
labelled as contested and given their serious range.

## 2. Audience and teaching pattern

A smart, busy, non-technical professional (a manager, analyst, or teacher). Every
concept is taught in three layers, in this order on the page:

| Layer | Treatment in the page |
|---|---|
| **Analogy** | A short set-off paragraph at the top of each chapter, labelled "Think of it this way". |
| **Foundation** | The main prose: why it works that way, in plain language. |
| **Deeper** | A native `<details>` panel labelled "Go deeper", with the math and the research. |

Each chapter also has at least one **predict-then-reveal**: the reader commits an
answer before the page shows the evidence.

## 3. Narrative beats and interactions

### Opening: "It Sounds Right."

- **Beat.** A parent asks what new discoveries from the Webb telescope they can tell a
  nine-year-old about. Two short answers appear in identical type. The reader must
  pick the one they would repeat to the child before the page continues.
- **The answers are real, not staged.** Answer A is Google's Bard chatbot, verbatim
  from Google's own February 2023 launch demo. Answer B is the opening of NASA's
  September 2022 announcement, written by people at NASA. The Bard answer claims Webb
  took "the very first pictures of a planet outside of our own solar system"; the
  first such picture was taken in 2004. NASA's sentence says Webb took its own first
  direct image. The two are a few words apart.
- **Reveal.** The machine's answer changes into the machine typeface with its
  shimmer; the human answer stays steady. The error is marked, with the correction
  and sources. Then the thesis, in one sentence.
- **Case file:** which answer the reader trusted.

*Why this pairing:* the brief asks for "one written by a person, one generated by an
AI", and forbids invented quotes. Writing both answers ourselves would mean passing
off machine text as human. Using a documented public error keeps everything real and
checkable, and it happens to show the mechanism the next chapter explains.

### Chapter 1: The Prediction Machine

- **Analogy:** an autocomplete that has read a library, or an improv actor who never
  says "I don't know".
- **History hook:** in 1948 Claude Shannon built a word-pair model by hand, flipping a
  book open at random pages. He wrote that "the labor involved becomes enormous at the
  next stage." The reader's browser does that next stage in about half a second.
- **Interactive (sticky graphic with five steps):**
  1. The model trains live on three Gutenberg books, with a visible count of words
     and patterns.
  2. *Predict:* "Which word most often follows 'I am' in these books?" (sure / not /
     afraid / sorry). Then the bar chart reveals it. ("sure" wins at about 14%, which
     is a nice irony in an essay about confidence.)
  3. A text box: type any start; the chart of the ten most likely next words updates
     live, with the tallies behind each bar. Click a bar (or press Enter on it) to add
     the word and build a sentence.
  4. Temperature slider from 0.1 to 3.0, marked repetitive / as trained / creative /
     nonsense. The bars reshape; an "effective choices" readout (2^entropy) makes the
     change legible.
  5. "Generate five times": five continuations of the same start, all in the same
     confident typeface.
- **Bridge to real LLMs:** vastly larger (the model here read about 150,000 words;
  Meta's Llama 3 was trained on over 15 trillion tokens), neural rather than a lookup
  table, and tuned with human feedback afterwards. The core move is the same: GPT-4's
  own report calls it "a Transformer-based model pre-trained to predict the next token
  in a document." Nothing in that objective checks truth.
- **Case file:** the prediction, and the sentence the reader built.

### Chapter 2: Confidently Wrong

- **Analogy:** a weather forecaster. "70% chance of rain" is well calibrated if it
  rains on about 70% of those days. The forecaster's tone tells you nothing; the track
  record does.
- **Interactive (predict-then-reveal throughout):** ten two-choice questions, each with
  a confidence slider from 50% (coin flip) to 100% (certain). Each answer is revealed
  with a one-line explanation and source. At the end the reader's calibration curve is
  plotted (their points in the reader color, the diagonal as the ideal), with average
  confidence, accuracy, the gap, and a Brier score.
- **The machine side:** OpenAI's GPT-4 report says the pre-trained model was "highly
  calibrated" and post-training reduced it; models that state confidence in words tend
  to overstate it; longer explanations raise human trust without raising accuracy.
- **Why hallucinations happen** (mechanism): no built-in truth check; the exam
  incentive (tests that don't penalize guessing train guessers, per Kalai et al. 2025);
  and people rating confident answers well.
- **Explorable:** "Grade the machine." The reader first predicts which strategy wins when
  wrong answers cost nothing. Then a slider (in twelfths of a point, so the tie at
  exactly one third is reachable) sets the penalty; unit grids show 100 questions for a
  model that always guesses and one that says "I don't know", and score bars compare
  them. With no penalty, bluffing wins, 70 to 60.
- **What reduces hallucination, and why:** grounding in sources (an open-book exam
  instead of a closed one), asking for citations (so checking becomes cheap), and
  separate verification steps. Each comes with its limits: legal research tools built
  on retrieval still hallucinated 17–33% of the time.
- **Also measured:** GPT-4's own report gives expected calibration error of 0.007 before
  post-training and 0.074 after, which the text turns into "under one percentage point"
  versus "more than seven".
- **Case file:** average confidence, accuracy, gap, Brier score (a verdict only after
  five answers).

### Chapter 3: The Jagged Frontier

- **Analogy:** a new colleague who drafts a clean memo in a minute, translates a letter
  and aces a PhD-level exam, but can't reliably read the clock on the wall. (The 2026 AI
  Index pairs an Olympiad gold with 50.6% on clocks.)
- **Interactive (predict-then-reveal):** a ledger of twelve tasks (not a card grid), each
  with a "does well / struggles" toggle and a sticky toolbar. Reveal: each row shows the
  research verdict, a one-line reason, a caveat and its sources. The score separates
  over-trust (expected it to do well; it struggles) from under-trust, because they cost
  different things.
- **Figure:** "One experiment, two sides of the frontier", using the paper's per-group
  numbers (quality +38% and +42.5%; correctness 84.5% without AI, 70.6% with GPT-4,
  60.0% with GPT-4 plus a prompting overview, read from its Figure 7).
- **Second predict-then-reveal:** a one-question poll on the METR developer trial.
- **The research:** Dell'Acqua et al.'s study of 758 BCG consultants. Inside the
  frontier, AI users finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with over 40% higher
  quality. On a similar-looking task outside it, they were 19 percentage points less
  likely to get the right answer. Plus the customer-support study (novices gain most)
  and METR's trial (experienced developers were 19% slower but believed they were 20%
  faster).
- **Why jagged:** models see chunks of text, not letters; abilities follow training
  data, not human difficulty.
- **Card selection rule:** only stable patterns with a current source. Two candidate
  cards were cut during research (see section 8).

### Chapter 4: The Trust Map

- **Analogy:** how you already treat a GPS: ignore it on the commute, follow it in a
  strange city, never onto a frozen lake.
- **Interactive:** a 2×2 map. X axis: how easy the output is to check. Y axis: how bad
  a mistake would be. The reader places twelve tasks by dragging, or with the keyboard
  (Enter selects a task, a quadrant menu places it, arrow keys then nudge it). Each
  quadrant carries its guidance: *Delegate freely*, *Use, then verify*, *Use for ideas
  only*, *Keep it human, or add expert review*. The machine quadrant is tinted jade and
  the human quadrant ochre, matching the essay's two signal colors.
- **Predict-then-reveal:** "Compare with ours" draws our suggested placement for each
  task as a hollow marker with a connecting line and the reasoning. The text says
  plainly that these are judgments, not facts.
- **Principle:** use AI where checking is cheaper than doing.
- **"How to work with it" panel:** give context; ask for sources and open them; ask it
  to argue against itself; check the parts that matter. Each has a one-line reason
  grounded in the mechanism (conditioning, cheap verification, sycophancy, uneven
  error).
- **Also:** the Mata v. Avianca sanctions opinion ("nothing inherently improper about
  using a reliable artificial intelligence tool… but existing rules impose a gatekeeping
  role") and automation bias (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010; Lee et al. 2025).
- **Case file:** the reader's placements; the rules card lists the tasks they kept human.

### Chapter 5: When Trust Scales

- **Analogy:** training a new recruiter by having them shadow ten years of decisions
  without saying which habits to avoid, then having them read ten thousand applications
  a day.
- **Opening case:** Reuters on Amazon's scrapped recruiting tool, including "no guarantee
  that the machines would not devise other ways of sorting candidates".
- **Centerpiece (sticky graphic, five steps):** a synthetic hiring history with two
  groups that have identical qualifications by construction, a past that favoured
  group A, and a town where most of group B lives in Eastfield. A logistic-regression
  screener (written from scratch) is trained without the group column.
  - *Predict:* will the screener treat the groups equally?
  - Reveal: selection rates, the impact ratio against the traditional 0.8 line, and
    the learned weights. The zip code carries the bias.
  - Toggle the zip code off: the ratio returns close to 1, while "agreement with past
    decisions" drops. Accuracy against a biased past rewards repeating it.
  - A counterfactual explanation for one real applicant in the demo: moving them
    across town, and nothing else, would have advanced them.
  - Honest scope: synthetic data, one proxy; real data has many subtle proxies, and
    fairness definitions can conflict.
- **Three dilemmas:** confidential data in a public chatbot (privacy), auto-sent
  customer replies (oversight), an unexplainable loan denial (accountability). The
  reader chooses; the page shows what each choice gains and risks, with a real case
  (Samsung 2023, Moffatt v. Air Canada 2024, Regulation B and the EU Court of Justice
  in 2025).
- **Principles mapped to mistakes:** fairness, privacy, transparency, accountability,
  human oversight, security, each paired with the mistake it prevents.
- **Case file:** the bias prediction and the three dilemma choices.

### Chapter 6: The Moving Boundary

All content comes from `src/content/timely.json` with "last checked" stamps.

- **Capability:** METR's time-horizon series as a log-scale chart, with the 50% and 80%
  reliability lines. *Predict:* the reader guesses the 2026 value on a log slider
  before the line is drawn. The gap between the two lines is the calibrated-trust
  point: what AI can do half the time is several times longer than what it does
  reliably.
- **Cost:** Epoch AI's lowest price at fixed performance, two step lines on a log
  scale, plus the September 2026 update.
- **From answers to actions:** why agents change the question from "is this answer
  right?" to "should this system be allowed to act?". An answer passes through your
  judgment; an action may not. Real incident, OWASP's risks, and a third axis for the
  trust map: can it be undone?
- **Rules:** the EU timeline (with the postponed dates struck through), the US federal
  and state picture, China's labelling rule.
- **The half-life of a fact:** eleven claims about AI with the date each was stated and
  when a source recorded it being overtaken. *Predict:* for each claim the reader says
  "still true" or "no longer true", and its lifeline grows on a shared time axis. The
  median lifespan (about 14 months, an upper bound) is computed from the data, not
  asserted. One claim is "revised" rather than overtaken and is not scored.

### Close: What We Keep

- Three scenarios, drawn from the International AI Safety Report 2026 and its OECD
  scenario work, each clearly labelled as a scenario rather than a prediction, with
  signposts to watch. Contested evidence on jobs, timelines, and extreme risk is shown
  side by side.
- A reflective turn on skills that fade without practice (pilots and autopilot,
  endoscopists and AI), the value of struggle in learning (the PNAS tutoring study), and
  attention and judgment (confidence in AI tracks less critical thinking). The reader
  chooses what they want to keep doing themselves.
- **"My Rules for AI" card:** assembled from the case file (calibration, frontier score
  with over- and under-trust, tasks kept human on the trust map, dilemma choices,
  keep-list) with three editable rules. The suggested rules follow the case file until
  the reader edits one. It downloads as a PNG drawn on a canvas (with the image shown
  inline as a fallback) and copies as plain text.
- **Analogy:** the calculator, which made a sense of the right answer more valuable, not
  less.

## 4. Engagement mechanics

- **Sticky graphics** (Chapters 1 and 5): the figure is pinned while step text scrolls
  past. Each step is also reachable with Previous/Next buttons and the left/right
  arrow keys while the figure has focus. Steps are ordinary sections, so the text is
  complete without scrolling or script.
- **Case file:** a drawer (bottom right) that collects answers and scores and shows a
  running count. It persists in `localStorage` (wrapped in try/catch; the page works
  without it) and can be reset.
- **Chapter navigator:** a slim bar across the top with a reading progress line. On wide
  screens it shows chapter numbers with the current chapter named (hover or focus names
  the others); below 1240px it collapses to the current chapter plus a menu, and on
  phones the wordmark shrinks to the essay's two marks.
- **Predict-then-reveal in every chapter:** the opening pick; the "I am" guess; ten
  calibration questions and the grading prediction; the frontier sort and the METR
  poll; the trust map and "compare with ours"; the screener prediction and dilemmas;
  the time-horizon guess and "still true?".

## 5. Visual design

### Direction

A premium Sunday-magazine long-read: a paper-white page, deep ink, a narrow reading
column, and full-bleed "instrument" panels for the graphics. Two voices are visible in
the typography itself: the **machine** speaks in a typewriter-like monospace with a
faint probability shimmer; the **reader's** marks are warm ochre, like pencil in a
case file.

**Deliberate departures from the obvious version.** The brief's direction (off-white
paper, an editorial serif, a warm human color) can easily slide into a stock look:
cream background, Fraunces headlines, a terracotta accent. To avoid it:

- Paper-white (`#faf9f5`) rather than cream.
- **Bodoni Moda** for headlines: a high-contrast Didone with optical sizes, which gives
  the magazine-cover register the brief asks for. It is used only at display sizes,
  where Didones are at their best.
- The reader's color is **ochre** (manila folder, pencil, lamplight) rather than
  terracotta.
- The machine's color is **jade**, a cool signal color that is neither the blue/purple
  of "AI" branding nor a circuit-board green.

### Tokens

| Token | Light | Dark | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| `--paper` | `#faf9f5` | `#141413` | page background |
| `--paper-2` | `#f1efe8` | `#1c1c1a` | panels, graphic stages |
| `--paper-3` | `#e7e4db` | `#262623` | tracks, sunken wells |
| `--ink` | `#191a1c` | `#e6e4dc` | body text (16.5:1 / 14.5:1) |
| `--ink-2` | `#45464b` | `#b3b1a8` | secondary text |
| `--ink-3` | `#6a6b70` | `#8a8880` | captions, axis labels (≥ 4.6:1) |
| `--rule` | `#dedbd2` | `#2c2c29` | hairlines, gridlines |
| `--machine` | `#08856a` | `#33a987` | machine marks |
| `--machine-ink` | `#16674f` | `#61c8a4` | machine-colored text accents (6.5:1 / 9.0:1) |
| `--human` | `#ad721c` | `#c3862e` | reader marks |
| `--human-ink` | `#7b510d` | `#deae62` | reader-colored text, focus ring (6.6:1 / 9.1:1) |

The machine/human pair was validated with the data-viz palette validator (OKLab ΔE,
Machado 2009 color-vision-deficiency simulation) against both surfaces:

- light: CVD ΔE 8.9, normal-vision ΔE 18.2, both marks ≥ 3:1 on paper;
- dark: CVD ΔE 10.8, normal-vision ΔE 18.1, both marks ≥ 3:1.

Color is never the only carrier of meaning. The machine is also monospace; reader
marks are filled circles and machine marks are squares or lines; correct and incorrect
use ✓ and ✗ with words; every chart has a text description and a table view.

### Type

| Role | Face | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Display | Bodoni Moda (variable, optical size 6–96) | H1, chapter titles, pull quotes |
| Text | Newsreader (variable, optical size 6–72) | body at 20px (19px on phones), line-height 1.6 |
| Machine / data | IBM Plex Mono 400/500/600 | model output, chart labels, UI labels (small caps tracking) |

Fonts are self-hosted from `src/assets/fonts` (Latin, plus Latin Extended for the body
face so citation names like "Budzyń" render), so the page makes no third-party
requests at all.

Scale (rem at 20px root on desktop): 0.7 / 0.8 / 0.9 / 1 / 1.25 / 1.6 / 2.2 / 3.2 /
clamp(3rem, 9vw, 6.5rem) for the masthead.

### Layout

- Reading column: `min(38rem, 100%)`, centered, with a 16px minimum gutter on every
  side at every width.
- Wide figures: `min(68rem, 100%)`. Sticky scrollytelling uses a two-column grid
  above 960px (graphic 60%, steps 40%) and stacks on phones: the graphic is pinned at
  the top at roughly half the screen height and the steps scroll beneath it.
- No horizontal page scroll anywhere; wide tables scroll inside their own container.

### Signature visual: the probability shimmer

Machine text is rendered one token per span. Each span carries `--p`, the probability
the model gave that word when it chose it. Two things show it:

- a dotted underline whose opacity rises as the word gets less likely (static, so it
  also works with reduced motion and in print), and
- a slow opacity flicker whose depth rises as the word gets less likely.

The reader learns across the piece that the machine's text looks equally steady
whether a word was a near-certainty or a long shot, and that the shimmer is how this
page makes the hidden odds visible. Where we don't have real probabilities (Bard's
answer in the opening), the shimmer is uniform and the caption says it is stylistic.

### Motion

One orchestrated moment per chapter at most: the opening reveal, the live bars, the
research rows settling in after the frontier sort, the lifelines growing in Chapter 6.
Everything else is still. With
`prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, all transitions are instant, the shimmer stops, and
smooth scrolling is off. Nothing is hidden at rest waiting for an animation.

## 6. Component architecture

```
confident-machine/
  index.html                 page shell; pulls in src/html/*.html via a tiny Vite plugin
  src/html/                  the essay's prose, one partial per chapter
  src/main.ts                boot: theme, navigator, case file, citations, lazy chapter mounts
  src/engine/                pure, tested logic (no DOM)
    rng.ts                   seeded mulberry32 + normal draws
    tokenizer.ts             word/punctuation tokens, display casing
    ngram.ts                 trigram model with Witten–Bell smoothing
    sampling.ts              temperature, entropy, sampling, top-k
    logistic.ts              logistic regression, log loss, AUC
    hiring.ts                synthetic world, screener, fairness metrics, counterfactuals
    calibration.ts           calibration curve, Brier score, summary
  src/lib/                   small DOM helpers
    dom.ts                   element builder, escaping-safe text
    store.ts                 case-file store with subscribe + localStorage
    scrolly.ts               sticky steps: IntersectionObserver + buttons + keys
    motion.ts                reduced-motion helper
    citations.ts             numbers [data-cite] markers, builds the source list
    timely.ts                typed access to timely.json, "last checked" stamps
    format.ts                numbers, dates, durations
    announce.ts              one polite live region for screen readers
  src/components/            shimmer text, chart helpers, case file, navigator, theme toggle
  src/chapters/              one module per chapter; each exports mount(root)
  src/content/               timely.json, sources.ts, and chapter data (quiz, cards, tasks)
  src/corpus/                cleaned Gutenberg texts + manifest (committed)
  tests/                     Vitest: engines + content integrity
  scripts/                   corpus prep, font copy, fact-freshness check, screenshots
```

Principles:

- **Engines are pure** and fully unit-tested; chapters only render.
- **Prose lives in HTML**, so the essay reads without JavaScript. Interactives
  enhance placeholders.
- **No framework.** Vanilla TypeScript with two D3 modules (`d3-scale`, `d3-shape`)
  for charts. SVG for all charts; canvas only for the rules-card PNG.
- **Lazy work:** the corpus (about 800 KB of text) is a separate chunk imported when
  Chapter 1 approaches the viewport, and the model trains in stages with progress
  shown.
- **One source of truth for facts:** static citations in `sources.ts`, time-sensitive
  claims in `timely.json`. Tests check that every citation used in the HTML exists,
  that every timely entry has a source URL and valid dates, and the
  `check:facts` script flags entries not re-checked within 120 days.

## 7. Accessibility

- Every control is a native element (`button`, `input type=range`, `select`,
  `details`) with a visible label. Focus rings use the reader color at 2px with offset,
  visible in both themes.
- Drag-and-drop on the trust map has a full keyboard path: Tab to a task, Enter to
  select it, a menu to drop it into a quadrant, then arrow keys to nudge it.
- Charts are SVG groups with an `aria-label`; each data point is focusable with its own
  label and tooltip; text beside or under each chart states the result in words; each
  chart has a "Show as table" toggle.
- An end-to-end keyboard-only run (skip link, every interactive, the case file drawer,
  the PNG download and copy) passes with no console errors.
- One polite `aria-live` region announces the results of interactions (reveals, scores).
- Color is never the only encoding (see section 5).
- `prefers-reduced-motion` and `prefers-color-scheme` are respected; a theme toggle
  overrides the system setting in both directions.

## 8. Research decisions, assumptions, and what could not be verified

**Decided during research**

- **Opening texts are verbatim and real.** Bard's answer was transcribed from the
  frames of Google's own launch video (blog.google, Feb. 6, 2023). NASA's sentence is
  from its Sept. 1, 2022 announcement. Market reaction (Alphabet −7.7%, about $100B)
  is CNN's figure.
- **The four-fifths rule is presented as a traditional benchmark, not current law.**
  In June 2026 the US Justice Department declared the 1978 Uniform Guidelines
  unlawful, and OPM removed them from federal hiring rules (July 2026), although the
  text remains in the eCFR. NYC Local Law 144 still requires publishing impact ratios.
  The demo keeps the 0.8 line because it is the long-standing yardstick and the
  metric itself is sound; the text says its legal status is contested.
- **EU dates reflect the July 2026 amendment.** Many web pages still say high-risk
  rules apply from August 2026. The AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force
  July 27, 2026) moved them to December 2027 and August 2028. We cite the European
  Commission directly.
- **Cut from the Chapter 3 cards: "detect AI-written essays".** The 2023 evidence
  (Liang et al.; OpenAI withdrawing its classifier) was strong, but a February 2026
  study of Czech-language detectors found no systematic bias against non-native
  writers. The pattern is no longer clearly stable, so it moved to the half-life
  section as a *revised* claim.
- **Cut from the Chapter 3 cards: "multiply two large numbers without a
  calculator".** Evidence is from 2023 models, and current reasoning models may have
  moved it. The clock-reading result (March 2026) makes the same point with current
  data.
- **The METR developer study is framed as early-2025 tools.** Its result may not hold
  for later tools; the text dates it.
- **The bias demo world was tuned for clarity, not for a result.** With 80/20
  neighbourhood segregation the effect was real but its size varied by random seed
  (impact ratio 0.51–0.76). We raised segregation to 85/15, which gives 0.44–0.66
  across eleven seeds; removing the zip code gives 0.82 or higher on every seed. The
  unit tests check the claim on seven seeds, not one.

**Could not verify, so written around**

- OpenAI's figure that its 2023 classifier caught 26% of AI-written text: the primary
  page was not reachable. The text says only that OpenAI withdrew it for "low rate of
  accuracy" (TechCrunch, July 2023).
- A federal court blocking Colorado's original AI Act was mentioned in secondary
  sources but not confirmed; the text only states the replacement law and its
  effective date (Hunton, May 2026).
- Exact token probabilities for Bard's 2023 answer are unknowable, so its shimmer is
  labelled as stylistic.
- The OECD scenario report itself (OECD 2026) was not read directly; the scenarios
  are cited from the International AI Safety Report 2026, which reproduces them.

**Assumptions**

- **Location in the repo.** The repository root holds an unrelated 2019 iOS project,
  so the essay lives in `confident-machine/`. Root-level `vercel.json` and a GitHub
  Pages workflow build from that folder, so it deploys as-is.
- **Authorship.** The text and code were drafted with an AI assistant. The colophon
  says so, which is on theme: every claim links to a source so readers can check it.
- **"Today" is 2026-09-27**, the research date. Every time-sensitive fact shows its
  own "last checked" date; the header shows the build date.
- **Corpus size.** To keep the bundle small, the library is all of *Alice's
  Adventures in Wonderland*, the first 34 chapters of *Pride and Prejudice*, and the
  first seven Sherlock Holmes stories: about 146,000 words and 800 KB of text.
- **Calibration questions deliberately include surprises.** Calibration depends on
  question difficulty (the hard–easy effect). The "Go deeper" panel says so, so the
  reader's result is treated as a snapshot, not a verdict.
