U.S. household wealth · 1913–2026

A country of two economies

American wealth is not merely unequal — it is unequal on a scale most charts cannot physically render, and it has been pulling apart for roughly fifty years. This page uses primary data from the Federal Reserve, the World Inequality Database, the IRS and federal campaign-finance filings to show how far apart the two economies have drifted, how fast, and how the rules that govern that gap have been rewritten.

Part one

The scale

Start with a single moment in time. Split every American household into four groups by net worth, then ask two questions: how many people are in each group, and how much of the country's wealth do they hold?

Why an average, and why it matters here

These are group averages — total wealth divided by the number of households in the group. For the bottom half that average is generous: it silently nets out the roughly one in ten American households with negative net worth against those with a little equity, so the typical household near the bottom holds less than the number shown. For the top 0.1% the average is conservative in a different way: within that sliver the distribution is itself extremely skewed, so most of its members hold far less than the average and a few hold vastly more. Averages flatten both tails. The gap below is the floor, not the ceiling.

Part two

The long arc, 1913–2024

Concentration of wealth is not a one-way ratchet. Over the last century it traced a deep U: a Gilded-Age peak, a half-century collapse, and a climb back that began around 1978 and has not stopped. The shape of that U tracks the rules — tax rates, labor law, financial regulation, campaign-finance law — more closely than it tracks which party held the White House.

Read this as correlation, not proof

Nothing on this chart demonstrates that a tax cut caused a rise in wealth concentration. Wealth shares move with asset prices, demographics, technology and global capital as well as with policy, and the two series shown move on different clocks — a tax change lands in a year, a wealth share responds over a decade. What the chart does establish is the association, and it is strong and sustained: the half-century when top rates sat above 70% is the same half-century when the top 1% share fell by more than half, and the turn upward begins within a few years of the turn downward in rates. The honest claim is that policy is one major input among several, and that it is the one input voters actually choose.

Part three

The modern surge, 1989–2026

Since 1989 the Federal Reserve has published a quarterly estimate of who owns America. It is the highest-resolution picture available, and it shows the gap widening through booms, busts, recoveries and both parties' administrations — interrupted only briefly, and never reversed.

That cliff in the green line is real

Between 2007 and 2011 the total net worth of the bottom half of American households fell from about $1.37 trillion to $253 billion — an 82% wipeout — and their share of national wealth fell to 0.4%. The reason is composition, not bad luck. Wealth at the bottom is almost entirely home equity, held with leverage: a 25% fall in house prices against an 80% mortgage erases most of the owner's stake. Wealth at the top is held in equities and business interests, unleveraged, and those assets were bought back up by a decade of cheap money. Both groups lost in 2008. Only one owned the assets that the recovery was designed to lift. It took the bottom half until 2019 — twelve years — merely to return to where it started, and even now its share of national wealth is lower than it was in 1989.

Part four

Money and power

Wealth concentration and political influence are not separate stories. Over the same decades that the top share climbed, the legal ceiling on political spending was dismantled piece by piece, the cost of running for federal office multiplied, and the share of that cost supplied by a few hundred households rose to something no previous era would recognize.

The mechanism, stated plainly

There is no need to allege a conspiracy, and the data above does not show one. The mechanism is structural and entirely legal: political campaigns cost money; the Supreme Court has held since Buckley (1976) that spending money on political speech is itself protected speech; after Citizens United and SpeechNow (both 2010) that spending became effectively unlimited for independent groups; the households able to supply money at that scale are, by definition, the households at the top of the distribution shown in Part One; and the policies most valuable to them — marginal rate cuts, capital-gains preferences, the carried-interest rule, estate-tax exemptions, weaker antitrust and labor enforcement — are precisely the policies that compound wealth at the top. Each turn of that loop makes the next turn easier to finance. That is what the two halves of this page have in common.

Part five

Sources and method

Every number on this page comes from a primary statistical source or a named published analysis. Nothing is modeled, smoothed or estimated by this page beyond the three arithmetic steps described below.

What was computed here, and how

1. Group averages. The Fed publishes each group's total net worth in dollars, not per-household figures. Average net worth per household = (group's total net worth) ÷ (total U.S. households × the group's fixed population share: 0.1%, 1%, 9%, 40%, 50%). Household counts come from the Census Bureau, interpolated from annual to quarterly and, for the most recent quarter, extended one year at the prior year's growth rate.

2. Inflation adjustment. Dollar figures are deflated by CPI-U (all urban consumers, not seasonally adjusted for quarterly averaging) and expressed in the dollars of the most recent quarter shown.

3. Indexing. The 1989 = 100 chart divides each group's real average net worth by its own 1989 Q3 value. That removes level differences so growth rates can share one axis — which is the correct alternative to plotting two wildly different dollar scales against two y-axes, a construction that manufactures apparent correlation out of arbitrary scale choices.

Two different measures of “the top 1%”

Part Two and Part Three use different sources and therefore report different numbers for the same concept — roughly 35% versus roughly 32% in recent years. This is not an error, and the two are not mixed in any single chart. The World Inequality Database series (Part Two) distributes wealth across adult individuals, splitting couples' assets, and is built by capitalizing income reported on tax returns back into the assets that produced it. The Fed's Distributional Financial Accounts (Part Three) distribute wealth across households, and are built by fitting Survey of Consumer Finances microdata to the aggregate national balance sheet. Different unit, different method, different number. Both show the same direction and roughly the same slope, which is the substantive point; a gap of a few percentage points between two honest methods is the normal state of distributional statistics, and any presentation that hides it is the one to distrust.

Caveats worth holding onto

Distributional wealth statistics are estimates, not counts. The Fed's accounts interpolate between triennial surveys; the capitalized-income method assumes a stable relationship between reported income and underlying assets, an assumption Kopczuk, Smith–Zidar–Zwick and others have challenged — their alternative estimates put the top 1% share somewhat lower, though still rising. The Saez–Zucman effective-tax-rate figures are contested by economists at AEI and elsewhere over the treatment of corporate tax incidence and transfer income. Dark-money totals are lower bounds by construction, since the money is undisclosed. None of these disputes changes the direction or the rough magnitude of the trends on this page; several change the decimal places.
